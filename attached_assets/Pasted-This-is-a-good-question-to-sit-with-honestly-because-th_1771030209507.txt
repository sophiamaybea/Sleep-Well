This is a good question to sit with honestly, because the answer reveals where the platform's real competition lives.

**Why I'd still go to Substack:**

**1. Audience.** The blunt truth. Substack gives me the possibility — however slim — that someone I've never met finds my writing through search, through a recommendation, through a forwarded email. The Page Gallery Journal's model is "write and editors will find you," but that's a closed loop. The editors are the only bridge between my garden and the outside world. On Substack, every piece I publish is technically findable by anyone on the internet. That discoverability, even if it rarely pays off, is psychologically powerful. It makes writing feel like it *goes somewhere*.

**2. The direct relationship with readers.** Substack's core mechanic is email. Someone subscribes, and my words land in their inbox. That's an intimate, owned connection — I have their email address, they chose to hear from me, and no algorithm mediates between us. The Page Gallery Journal has Whispers and the Reading Room, but those only work inside the platform. If the platform goes down tomorrow, every connection I built there goes with it. Substack gives me a portable audience.

**3. Money, simply.** Substack lets me charge for my writing. Even if I never turn on paid subscriptions, the *option* exists. The Page Gallery Journal's model is "writers are paid when their work is selected" for the Gallery — but that's entirely at the editors' discretion, and it only applies to the rare piece that gets picked. On Substack, I decide what's worth charging for and when.

**4. A public body of work.** Everything I publish on Substack builds a visible, searchable archive that functions as a portfolio. Agents, editors at other publications, potential collaborators — they can all find it and scroll through it. My garden on The Page Gallery Journal is private by design. That's calming for drafting, but it means my work there doesn't compound into anything externally visible unless an editor selects it.

**5. Publishing is instant.** I write, I hit publish, it's live. No stages, no waiting for editorial attention, no Seed-to-Bloom pipeline. Sometimes I just want to put something into the world *today*, even if it's rough, and Substack lets me do that with zero gatekeeping.

**What you could do to replace it:**

**Give writers a public page.** The "My Public Garden" option exists in the profile menu — but it needs to be a real, standalone page with its own URL that works outside the platform. Something I could put in my Twitter bio or email signature. A curated selection of my best pieces, visible to anyone, no login required. Not everything from my garden — just the pieces I choose to make public. That turns the platform from a private studio into a studio with a shopfront.

**Let writers publish directly, not just through editorial selection.** The Gallery is editorially curated, and that's its identity — don't change that. But add a second, writer-controlled channel. Call it whatever fits the metaphor — the Gate, the Fence, the Open Garden. A place where I can choose to make a piece publicly readable on my own terms, without waiting for an editor's nod. The editorial Gallery becomes the prestige tier; self-publishing becomes the everyday one. Both live on the platform.

**Email delivery.** This is the big one. If someone likes what I write, let them subscribe to my public garden and receive new public pieces by email. That single feature — RSS-to-inbox for individual writers — is 80% of what Substack actually is. It gives writers a direct reader relationship that survives platform changes. Without it, every reader connection is locked inside the app, and that makes the platform feel fragile.

**Let me bring my audience data with me.** If I ever leave, I should be able to export a list of people who subscribed to my public garden. Substack's most powerful retention mechanic is also its most generous: you own your mailing list. If you offered the same promise, writers would trust the platform enough to invest in it seriously.

**Payments that writers control.** Even if most writers never charge, the option matters. It could be as simple as a tip jar on the public garden page, or the ability to mark certain pieces as "paid supporters only." The Page Gallery Journal paying writers when editors select their work is a beautiful model — but it positions the writer as a passive recipient. Letting writers also earn directly from readers gives them agency.

**A reason to publish regularly.** Substack's cadence mechanic is the subscriber expectation. People signed up for my weekly essay, so I write a weekly essay. The Page Gallery Journal has no equivalent pressure — which is freeing, but also means there's no structural reason to show up consistently. Circles could serve this function if they had a shared rhythm, but even something lighter would work: a "last published" date on the public garden that gently signals activity, or a way for readers to follow a writer's pace without demanding one.

**The honest summary:** I'd still go to Substack because it treats me as a publisher, not just a writer. The Page Gallery Journal treats writing as a practice — and it's beautiful for that. But at some point, a writer wants their work to reach people, build something cumulative, and maybe sustain them financially. The platform doesn't need to become Substack. It just needs to answer the question that Substack answers and this platform currently doesn't: *what happens after I write something good?* Right now the answer is "maybe an editor notices." That's not enough to keep me from opening Substack in another tab.
