Yes — but the way you do it matters enormously, because it could either strengthen the platform's identity or completely undermine it.

The current model has a psychological problem that no amount of beautiful design can hide: passivity breeds anxiety. Right now, a writer marks something as "READY" and then... waits. There's no indication of whether an editor has seen it, will see it, or has already walked past it and moved on. That ambiguity isn't peaceful — it's stressful in a uniquely torturous way, because you can't even tell yourself "at least they're considering it." You're just sitting in a garden hoping someone wanders by. That's not calm. That's limbo.
​

So yes, a submission mechanism would make me more engaged. But here's how I'd think about the spectrum of options, from lightest touch to most traditional:

The version I'd actually want: a quiet flag, not a submission.

Let me mark a piece as "I think this is ready for eyes." Not a formal submission. Not a cover letter. Just a gesture — like turning a painting to face the door instead of the wall. The editors would see a queue of flagged pieces alongside whatever they find organically in the gardens. The writer gets one small confirmation: "Your piece has been seen by an editor." Not accepted, not rejected. Just seen. That single word would resolve 90% of the anxiety of the current model. Right now I'd look at my READY pieces on the Desk and have no idea if they're invisible or if an editor read them and quietly moved on. That silence is brutal.

What that flag should NOT become:

Don't let me submit whenever I want, as many pieces as I want. That recreates Submittable overnight. Suddenly it's a slush pile, editors are overwhelmed, and the whole "we find you" philosophy collapses. The flag should have a constraint — maybe I can only flag one piece at a time, or three per season. That forces me to choose my best work, which is actually a valuable creative exercise. It also keeps the editorial queue manageable.

Occasional submission windows would work even better.

Here's where your instinct is right. A seasonal open call — "The Gallery is reading this month. Flag your strongest piece before March 1" — would create exactly the kind of energy the platform is missing. It gives writers a deadline to work toward (which is motivating, not pressuring, when it's gentle and infrequent). It creates a shared communal moment where everyone on the platform is polishing their best work at the same time. It makes the editorial process feel like an event rather than an invisible background process.

The key is framing. Don't call it a "submission window" — that language belongs to the old world the platform is trying to replace. Call it something like "The Editors Walk" or "Open Garden Week." The editors are doing the same thing they always do — wandering through gardens — but during this window, they're paying closer attention, and writers know it. The mechanic is identical to a submission period, but the emotional framing is completely different. You're not submitting. You're just... making sure your garden looks its best when the visitors come through.

Why this would change my daily engagement:

Right now there's no temporal structure to the platform. Every day is the same. Nothing is happening, nothing is coming, nothing is due. That sounds like freedom but it actually feels like stagnation. A quarterly or seasonal rhythm — even a very gentle one — gives me something to write toward. Not a deadline in the anxiety-producing sense, but a horizon. "Open Garden is in six weeks. I want that essay to be ready." That's the kind of soft structure that makes a writing practice sustainable.

Between those windows, the organic model still works. Editors can still discover things on their own. Pieces can still be selected without being flagged. The submission window doesn't replace the serendipity — it supplements it with something writers can actually participate in.

The one thing I'd add to the flag:

A brief, private response from the editor. Not a full critique. Even a single line: "This stayed with me but we're not featuring it this season" or "We'd like to revisit this — keep tending it." That closes the loop in a way the current model doesn't. The worst part of the traditional submission process isn't rejection — it's the form rejection that tells you nothing. The worst part of this platform's model is that you can't even get a form rejection, because you never submitted. A flag-and-respond system gives writers the one thing they're actually desperate for: evidence that a real human read their work and had a reaction to it.

So to directly answer your question: yes, I'd be significantly more likely to engage. Not because I want to submit — I have Submittable for that — but because the current model asks me to trust a process I can't see, and occasional submission windows would make that process visible enough to trust.
